# Third Peak — Content Style Guide

Voice: personal, direct, earned authority. The writing should feel like a senior practitioner thinking out loud — not like a briefing document, not like a LinkedIn post.

---

## The Non-Negotiables

### 1. No "It's Not X, It's Y"

The single most prevalent AI writing pattern. It shows up everywhere, in many forms:

- *"The ceiling, in other words, is not a demand problem. It's a delivery infrastructure problem."*
- *"This is not an argument against AI adoption. It's an argument about what kind of investment is durable."*
- *"The process observation phase isn't a delay to the AI project. It is the AI project."*
- *"It's not leverage. It's closer to a trade."*
- *"The risk isn't that AI gets better. The risk is that organizations have built dependencies."*

**Why it reads as AI:** Models use negative setup as a rhetorical shortcut to create the feeling of reframing. Human writers who've earned a conclusion just land on it. The negative clause is usually unnecessary — if the positive case is made well, the reader doesn't need to be told what it isn't.

**Fix:** Lead with what it IS. Cut the negative frame entirely, or fold the contrast into a single direct statement.

| Instead of | Write |
|---|---|
| *"It's not a demand problem. It's a delivery infrastructure problem."* | *"The ceiling comes from delivery infrastructure, not demand."* |
| *"This isn't a delay to the AI project. It is the AI project."* | *"Treat process observation as the beginning of the AI project."* |
| *"It's not leverage. It's closer to a trade."* | *"The net result is a trade, not leverage."* |

---

### 2. No "There Is a [Noun] Worth Noting"

AI throat-clearing. The model is deciding what to say next and announcing it before saying it.

- *"There is a specific discipline that has developed tools for exactly this problem."*
- *"There is a timing element worth naming directly."*
- *"There is also an effect on quality of engagement."*

**Fix:** Start with the noun doing something, not "there is."

| Instead of | Write |
|---|---|
| *"There is a specific discipline that has developed tools for this."* | *"Anthropologists and organizational ethnographers have spent decades developing tools for exactly this."* |
| *"There is a timing element worth naming directly."* | *"The timing matters."* |
| *"There is also an effect on quality of engagement."* | *"There's a quality-of-engagement effect here too."* |

---

### 3. No Meta-Commentary

Don't announce what you're about to say. Just say it.

- *"Consider what this means concretely."* → delete, go straight to the example
- *"This sounds abstract, so I want to be concrete about what it means in practice."* → delete
- *"I want to make a claim that runs against the current grain of AI adoption advice."* → just make the claim
- *"The framing I keep returning to is this:"* → just state the framing
- *"Here is what I actually recommend, and it is routinely the most surprising thing I say:"* → trim the self-commentary

---

### 4. No Hedging Qualifiers

Words that soften every claim into mush. Cut them or replace with specifics.

**Cut on sight:** *typically, generally, arguably, essentially, broadly speaking, in some ways, to some degree, tends to, largely, often*

**Exception:** When uncertainty is genuinely meaningful, say why. "In most of the firms I've worked with" is specific and earned. "Typically" is not.

---

### 5. No Escalator Phrases

- *"Now more than ever"*
- *"This matters more now than it ever has"*
- *"Has never been more important"*
- *"In today's rapidly changing landscape"*

These read as filler inserted to make a point sound urgent. If the point is urgent, the argument makes it urgent.

---

### 6. No "At Best… At Worst" Lists

An AI patterning device that presents a tidy polarity. Real-world implications are messier and more interesting than two-item poles.

**Fix:** Show the specific, concrete failure modes instead of categorizing them.

---

### 7. No Contrarian Positioning Language

- *"Against the current grain of AI adoption advice"*
- *"Runs counter to conventional wisdom"*
- *"The counterintuitive thing about X is…"*

If the idea is genuinely counterintuitive, the reader will feel that from the argument. Announcing that it's contrarian is hedging — it signals the writer isn't confident the argument carries itself.

---

### 8. No Disclaimer Paragraphs

*"This is not an argument against X. It's an argument about Y."*

If a reader might confuse the argument for something it isn't, that's a structuring problem to fix in the argument, not a caveat to add at the top.

---

### 9. No Template Closings

*"The organizations that navigate this well are the ones who…"* is a formula. So is *"The leaders who get this right will be…"* These signal that the writer has run out of specific things to say and has retreated to a category statement.

**Fix:** End on a specific idea, a specific question, or the sharpest sentence in the piece. If the last paragraph could be the last paragraph of any article, rewrite it.

---

### 10. "Paradoxically" — Show, Don't Name

If a thing is paradoxical, let the reader feel the paradox from the evidence. Naming it is telling, not showing.

---

## Word Cautions

**Cut on sight (AI filler/buzzwords):**  
*simultaneously, transformative, game-changing, revolutionize, seamless, leverage (as a verb), harness, illuminate, underscore, shed light on, delve into, pivotal, realm, bolster, streamline, cutting-edge, innovative*

**Watch carefully:**  
*essentially, effectively, actually* (often used as intensifiers that add nothing), *navigate* (overused as a metaphor for "handle"), *landscape*

**"Actually"** — useful only when correcting a specific misapprehension. "What's actually happening here" → "What's happening here."

---

## Structural Notes

**Sentence length variation:** Short sentences land harder when surrounded by longer ones. But a piece built on a rhythm of short-medium-short-emphatic becomes predictable fast. Vary without a formula.

**First person:** Use it assertively. "I recommend" beats "one might consider." Don't hide behind passive constructions or third-person generalizations when a personal observation is more honest.

**Section headings:** Should be interesting, not descriptive. A heading that could be an H2 in any article about AI isn't doing enough work.

**The ending:** The last sentence of the piece is the one readers carry with them. Don't let it be a summary. Let it be the sharpest version of the central idea.
